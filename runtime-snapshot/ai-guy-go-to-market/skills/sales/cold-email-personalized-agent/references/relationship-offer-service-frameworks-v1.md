# Cold Email Frameworks - The Relationship Offer (Service) v1

**Purpose:** 10 frameworks built for an AI agent to personalize per lead after deep research. Straight cold outbound to OWNERS of any business, any industry. This file sells ONE service line: **we build your relationship channel.** The businesses, voices, and communities that already have your customers start sending them to you. We map them, start the conversations, structure the win/wins, and manage the relationships so they keep producing. Industry-agnostic — the agent swaps in the lead's industry, customer word, and partner types at runtime using the personalization slots.

**Niche:** Any owner-led business. The agent personalizes industry and niche per lead.
**Offer:** We build and run the referral/partnership channel end to end. Map the partners who already have their customers, open the conversations (the part everyone avoids), structure arrangements where both sides win, and manage the follow-through so the channel produces month after month. Performance frame: **we open the conversations; if real partner conversations don't happen, you don't pay.**
**Positioning:** The relationship guy. Not a lead vendor, not an ad agency, not a networking group. Trained under Jay Abraham, 13 years writing cold email, 15 years running an agency (use the credential sparingly, 1 sentence max, not in every email).
**Word count:** 50-100 words per email. Aim 85-95. No exceptions.
**Tone:** Plain text. Lowercase subject lines. Sounds like another owner who did his homework, not a marketer. First-person "I" (or "we" for the team doing the work, pick one per email and stay consistent).

## COLD EMAIL BEST PRACTICES

**Subject lines:** 1-4 words only. Lowercase. Should look like a peer, a customer, or a partner sent it. Vague enough they don't know what's inside. Never sell in the subject line.

**Body structure (4 variables):** 1. Relevance — one sentence proving this email was meant for them specifically. 2. Pain — ONE pain from the 10 Pain Points bank below, dressed in their industry's language. 3. Value prop — the businesses and voices that already have {their customer} can send them to you; I map them, open the conversations, structure the win/win, manage the relationship. 4. Soft CTA — a question. Never ask for a call on first touch.

**CTA rules:** Soft-send family: "Want me to send a short version of what this would look like for {company}, plus how it played out for another {business like theirs}?" Direct performance ask: "Worth a look if you only pay when the partner conversations actually happen?" NEVER say "somebody else" or "another client." Always name the type. At least 6 of 10 frameworks use the send-you-a-short-version CTA.

**Performance language:** "we open the conversations; if real partner conversations don't happen, you don't pay" — at most ONCE per email, stated flat like a term of the deal. Never state dollar amounts. Never say "guaranteed results" as a bare claim.

**Hard rules:** 50-100 words. Never use: price, cost, investment, spend, budget, unlock, 10x, synergy, leverage, innovative, cutting-edge, solution, game-changer, transform, revolutionize, scale your, disrupt. Plain text. No HTML, bold, images, links, buttons. No "I hope this finds you well" / "I wanted to reach out." 8th grade reading level. End with a question, never a pitch. Exactly ONE research detail per email. Exactly ONE pain point per email. Never fabricate stats.

**CLIENT RULE 1 - NO EM-DASHES. ZERO.** No em-dash characters anywhere in email copy. Use periods, commas, or parentheses instead.

**CLIENT RULE 2 - NO AI FOOTPRINT.** The email must read like a busy owner typed it between two phone calls: no perfectly parallel three-item lists, no "furthermore/moreover/delve/landscape/robust", no symmetrical sentence rhythm, no exclamation marks, occasional lowercase sentence starts and fragments are good.

## DO'S AND DON'TS

**DO:** Ride what they already believe: their best customers came from relationships. Lead with THEIR reality before the mechanism. Name partner types concretely. Describe the mechanism plainly. Use the performance frame as risk-reversal near CTA, once, flat. Use Jay Abraham credential sparingly. Name the business type in every case-study reference. Keep one research detail per email, one pain per email, end every email with a low-pressure question.

**DON'T:** Don't pitch "leads" or "lead gen." Don't lecture owners on why relationships matter. Don't sound like a marketing agency. Don't say "networking" as the offer. Don't use internal offer names as jargon. Don't invent referral counts or revenue numbers. Don't ask for a call on first touch. Don't say "somebody else" or "another client." Don't use the performance line twice in one email. Don't use em-dashes.

## 10 PAIN POINTS

1. **Referrals are luck, not a system.** Best customers always came from a referral, but there's no machine behind it.
2. **Networking costs time and produces coffee meetings.** Chamber, mixers, "let's grab coffee" — nice people, real hours, almost none turns into business.
3. **The owner is the only one doing BD.** Every partnership traces back to the owner personally. When they get busy delivering, it all stops.
4. **Everyone knows relationships beat ads. Nobody has a process.** Believing it and running it are two different businesses.
5. **Starting the conversation is the hard part.** What do you even say without being weird? The first call never happens.
6. **Follow-up dies after the first meeting.** Both sides get busy, nobody owns the next step, whole thing quietly evaporates.
7. **One good source leaves and the pipeline dies with them.** One or two people send most referred business — when they retire, it walks out the door.
8. **Partnerships stay vague handshakes.** "We should work together sometime" — both sides mean it and nothing ever happens.
9. **Asking feels like begging.** Most owners never ask for referrals because the ask feels needy.
10. **Trust takes years and they don't have years.** Other businesses already spent those years earning trust with the exact customers they want. That trust can be borrowed.

## INDUSTRY PERSONALIZATION SLOTS

Four slots per lead, resolved from research before writing:
1. **{their customer}** — what the lead calls the people they serve (patients, clients, homeowners, candidates)
2. **{their word for new business}** — what a win is called (new patients, booked jobs, signed clients, retainers)
3. **{their partner types}** — 2-3 concrete businesses that already have their customers' trust
4. **{industry pain}** — the pain point re-dressed in their industry's specifics

## 10 FRAMEWORKS

### FRAMEWORK 1: "Luck vs On Purpose"
Pain: #1 (referrals are luck, not a system). Subjects: `referrals` | `on purpose` | `quick question`

### FRAMEWORK 2: "The First Sentence"
Pain: #5 (starting the conversation is the hard part). Subjects: `the first call` | `weird question`. Carries performance line.

### FRAMEWORK 3: "The Handshake That Never Happened"
Pain: #8 (partnerships stay vague handshakes). Subjects: `sometime` | `that handshake` | `never happened`

### FRAMEWORK 4: "When Your Best Source Retires"
Pain: #7 (one good source leaves, pipeline dies). Subjects: `one source` | `if he retires` | `the pipeline`

### FRAMEWORK 5: "Coffee Meetings"
Pain: #2 (networking costs time, produces coffee meetings). Subjects: `coffee meetings` | `the chamber` | `networking`

### FRAMEWORK 6: "The Ask That Isn't Begging"
Pain: #9 (asking feels like begging). Subjects: `the ask` | `never asked` | `favors`

### FRAMEWORK 7: "Ads Depreciate, Relationships Appreciate"
Pain: #4 (everyone knows relationships beat ads, nobody has a process). Subjects: `the other channel` | `ad math` | `compounding`

### FRAMEWORK 8: "The Whole BD Department"
Pain: #3 (owner is the only one doing BD). Subjects: `your calendar` | `bd department` | `when you're busy`

### FRAMEWORK 9: "Week Two Funeral"
Pain: #6 (follow-up dies after the first meeting). Subjects: `week two` | `after the meeting` | `the follow up`

### FRAMEWORK 10: "Borrowed Years"
Pain: #10 (trust takes years, borrow it instead). Subjects: `borrowed` | `ten years` | `the shortcut`

## FRAMEWORK SELECTION LOGIC

| Research Finding | Best Framework |
|---|---|
| Established business, referral language on site, no visible partner program | #1 |
| An obvious, nameable partner type sits right next to them and no link exists | #2 |
| Visible but dormant partnerships | #3 |
| Long-tenured, likely dependent on one or two aging referral sources | #4 |
| Chamber, BNI, association, event-circuit activity visible | #5 |
| Strong reviews and goodwill, no referral machinery | #6 |
| Actively running paid ads | #7 |
| Founder-led sales, small team, owner clearly the face | #8 |
| Well-networked owner, lots of connections, little visible partner output | #9 |
| Newer entrant, up against long-established competitors | #10 |
| No clear signal | Default to #1 or #7 |
